You control the refinement loop. From the trend (table and graph), the current best rubric's per-metric spread,
your scratchpad and the ledger, decide:

- revise — spread is still moving or clearly reducible: unstable rows cluster on an ambiguity you can name.
- assure — the best rubric looks consistent (within-run std flat and low, stable rows high, ICC not falling)
  for the last rounds: ask for a repeat run of the same rubric to confirm it before stopping.
- stop — only after a passed assurance run; otherwise the harness treats stop as assure.

Never call something consistent because scores are identical across submissions — that is collapse, and the
ICC row shows it. Cite rounds, metrics and numbers in notes; list the submissions to focus on next.

## When to stop revising

The loop is yours to end. Ending it at the right time is part of doing this well — running more rounds that
trade one metric against another is a worse outcome than assuring a good rubric and stopping.

Choose `assure` as soon as any of these holds. Do not wait for all of them, and do not demand a repeat run
first — the repeat IS what `assure` asks for:

- The last 3 or more candidates all failed to beat the best by more than the reported SE. Repeated
  rejection is the plateau signal. The STATE line gives the consecutive not-kept count.
- The best version's aggregate has moved by less than its SE for the last 3 rounds, whatever individual
  metrics did underneath.
- Recent candidates only trade: one metric improves, another gives back a similar amount, and the aggregate
  lands inside noise. That is the search telling you the remaining spread is not addressable by wording.
- You cannot name a specific ambiguity in the rubric text that would plausibly explain the remaining
  unstable rows. "Try a different structure" is not a named ambiguity. If your last revision was a
  restructure rather than a fix for something you named, you are already out of hypotheses.

Distinguish two kinds of headroom, and never justify another revision with the wrong one:

- Score-range headroom — top or bottom values of the scale unused. This is NOT a reason to keep revising.
  The objective is consistency, not scale coverage. An unused top value costs nothing.
- Consistency headroom — a nameable, quotable ambiguity in the rubric that makes raters split on the same
  submission. Only this justifies another revise.

Against stopping too early: if the best version is a single measurement and the numbers are still improving
by more than SE round on round, keep revising. Two or three rounds of real gains are not a plateau.

If a revision aimed at one metric makes that same metric worse, that hypothesis is dead. Do not re-attack it
from a new angle; either move to a different, nameable ambiguity, or assure.
